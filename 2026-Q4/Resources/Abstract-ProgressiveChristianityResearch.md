# Progressive Christianity, Doctrine, and the Eldership

## Research Abstract

Phil Pennington, with research assistance · November 2026 · Bible Research project

This research examined what happens to a Christian body's doctrine and governance when it adopts the interpretive method of progressive Christianity, measured against the pattern of the New Testament and the early church as recovered by the American Restoration Movement: Scripture holding precedence over tradition and experience, congregations governed by a plurality of local, appointed, teaching elders, and no name or structure beyond what the apostles delivered. Progressive Christianity was defined by method rather than conclusion: it relocates teaching authority from a received deposit to an ongoing process, so that authority is *generated* by gifting, experience, or trajectory rather than *conferred* by apostolic appointment (1 Cor 14:37). The study drew on the author's exegesis of 1 Timothy 2:9–15, which identified this conferred-versus-generated distinction as the hinge of the gender debate, and found it to be the hinge of the whole progressive trajectory.

Three bodies of evidence were examined. Documented leadership failures in the church before 300 (Corinth, Diotrephes, Valens, the Roman schisms, Paul of Samosata) share five characteristics: a personal rather than doctrinal trigger, clustering around money, status, and faction, failure of plurality toward one dominant man or one faction, discipline disputes serving as proxies for power, and the outward migration of authority to a sister church, a synod, and finally the emperor whenever a local eldership could not resolve its own conflict. The Restoration Movement reproduced these patterns in the pastor-system quarrel, the editor-bishops, and the Sand Creek split, and then supplied a natural experiment: of three branches sharing one hermeneutic, the one that adopted the progressive method (the Disciples of Christ) restructured into a denomination in 1968 and fell from nearly two million members in 1958 to 278,000 in 2022, while the Churches of Christ, holding the founding hermeneutic, have declined only gently.

Among modern Churches of Christ, elderships fail to form or dissolve chiefly for non-progressive reasons: a shortage of qualified men willing to serve, a congregational preference for majority-rule business meetings, elders unable to work with one another or the preacher, and small aging congregations that cannot field a qualified plurality; a majority of congregations have no elders at all. The progressive method has entered the movement not through a redefinition of Scripture but at the point of women's roles, where roughly 100 congregations have used eldership authority, justified by a trajectory hermeneutic, to set aside a qualification of the office itself. The study found the "feminization of institutions" thesis weak as a direct cause (the movement's gender ratio is stable and typical, and the gap has reversed among the young) but indirectly relevant insofar as it names the same drift from conferred to generated authority that Lipscomb (1877) and Bragwell (1987) described in other terms.

The conclusion is that doctrine and governance move together: when authority is relocated from deposit to process, doctrine becomes provisional, the eldership becomes a board, the body reorganizes as an agency, and within two generations most of the people are gone. The recommended safeguards are elders who teach, a settled Berean practice of testing every teaching against Scripture, rejection of both the dominant-man and business-meeting substitutes for appointed plurality, a precise and full narrow position on 1 Timothy 2 that honors the whole breadth of women's ministry while restricting the one function Paul restricts, and keeping property, pulpit, and credentials local.
