# The Last Eldership

### How a congregation loses its shepherds, and why it rarely notices until they are gone

Phil Pennington, with research assistance · November 2026 · Bible Research project

---

Every congregation that has lost its eldership can name the day it happened: the elders resigned together, or the last one died, or no one stood. What almost none can name is the day the eldership actually ended, because that day came years earlier and nothing visible changed. The men still met and the budget still passed. But the thing Paul described to the Ephesian elders, men whom "the Holy Spirit has made overseers" to guard the flock against wolves "from among your own group" (Acts 20:28–30), had already stopped existing. What remained was its outline.

The sequence is old. It ran in the second century and the nineteenth, and is running now. It has one root and five stages.

## The root

The root is a question: where does the authority to teach come from? Either it is *conferred*, delivered by appointment and handed down as a deposit to be guarded (2 Tim 2:2), or it is *generated*, produced by gifting, results, the will of the people, or the direction history seems to be moving. Paul answered in the passage that regulates the assembly: "If anyone considers himself a prophet or spiritual person, he should acknowledge that what I write to you is the Lord's command" (1 Cor 14:37). He did not deny the Corinthians their gifts. He denied that gifts adjudicate order.

No eldership ever voted to adopt generated authority. It is adopted the way a house settles, one reasonable accommodation at a time.

## Stage one: the elders stop teaching

The New Testament elder is first a teacher, "able to teach" (1 Tim 3:2) and to "refute those who contradict" (Titus 1:9). The first accommodation is the quiet transfer of teaching to the hired man, for good reasons: he is trained, he has the time. The elders keep buildings, budgets, and personnel, and within a decade the congregation has a preacher and a board and calls the board an eldership. The effect is not felt until a teaching arrives that the men have never had to answer, and they find they cannot, because refuting requires knowing and knowing was outsourced. A board can approve what the staff proposes. It cannot guard.

## Stage two: the vacuum fills

The space is always filled by one of three things. The first is a dominant man: John's Diotrephes, who "loves to be first" (3 John 9), or the located preacher who controls the pulpit and therefore the congregation. The second is an outside authority: Rome writing to Corinth, synods settling Roman elections, the emperor settling Antioch; in the movement, the editor-bishops and the denomination the Disciples voted themselves into in 1968. Each intervention solved a dispute and took from the congregation something Acts 14:23 had placed there, never returned. The third is the congregation itself, deciding by majority, which is generated authority in its purest form: the congregation decides what it believes and decides again when the majority changes.

## Stage three: the deposit becomes a process

With no one whose office it is to keep the deposit fixed, the deposit begins to change. In the Churches of Christ this is never announced as a change in the status of Scripture. It arrives as a change on one question, justified by one new argument: that the New Testament is moving in a direction and faithfulness means continuing past the text. But that is a method, not a conclusion, and a method does not stay on the question it was borrowed for. The bodies that used it to open the teaching office used it again, a generation later, to redefine marriage, and found no ground left to refuse, having conceded that an instruction Paul grounded in creation (1 Tim 2:13) could be set aside by trajectory. When an eldership appoints women as elders, it uses the authority conferred by the qualifications of 1 Timothy 3 to set aside one of them. The office is used to amend the terms of the office.

## Stage four: the church becomes an agency

A deposit is guarded by shepherds; a process is advanced by an organization. Once the congregation sees its purpose as advancing something rather than keeping something, it reorganizes around staff, programs, and a board. It needs no denomination to do this. It needs only to stop asking whether its elders teach and start asking whether its programs grow.

## Stage five: the last eldership

This is the stage the congregation can date. Qualified men decline an office that has become criticism without authority; the men who seek it should not have it; the congregation prefers a vote to overseers; the remaining elders cannot work together; the body has aged until it cannot field a qualified plurality. A majority of congregations in the movement now have no elders at all. None of these causes is heresy. That is the point. The last eldership is dissolved by the accumulated effects of having stopped, years earlier, being an eldership in Paul's sense. The early church's failures were triggered by envy, status, and money; the doctrine came later. The pattern has not changed.

## What cuts the root

Guard the hinge, not the list: hold conclusions because Scripture delivers them, not because they are traditional or popular, since those are revised when the tradition or the majority changes. Have elders who teach, who know the Word well enough to refute, and are supported so they can. Refuse both the dominant man and the business meeting; both are generated authority in different clothes. Hold the narrow position on 1 Timothy 2 precisely and in full, restricting the one function Paul restricts, grounding it where he grounds it, and honoring every form of women's ministry the New Testament records, so nothing is left for a trajectory argument to grip. And keep the building, the pulpit, and the credentials local.

"The Holy Spirit has made you overseers." The authority was conferred. It was not generated by the men who hold it and cannot be amended by them. An eldership that remembers this will not be the last one.
