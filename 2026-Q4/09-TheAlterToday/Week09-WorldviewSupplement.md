# Week 9 Supplement: Where Did I Learn to Think This Way?

*Worldview reflection for "The Altar Today: Living Sacrifice, Royal Priesthood." Companion to `Week09-TeacherGuide.md`. Scripture from the NET Bible; keep the course's usual translation in class.*

**What this adds (and nothing more):** one new movement inside Segment 4 and the discussion. The class already holds the hinge ("sacrifice changed material") and the convergence (Romans 12:1–2 = Hebrews 5:14). This supplement adds one more thing to carry out the door: *the body and the mind we are laying on the altar were made by God for a purpose, and each of us has been quietly shaped by sources that teach otherwise. Renewal begins by noticing who has been shaping us.*

**Guardrails stay lit.** (1) Mercy first, altar second. This is an examination offered in gratitude, never a payment. Renewal is something God does as we yield ("be transformed" is passive; Titus 3:5 speaks of "the renewal of the Holy Spirit"). (2) Week 5's Berean method is the *criterion*. Tracing where an idea came from helps us notice it; only Scripture tests it.

---

## 1. The exegetical backbone (for the teacher)

**Romans 12:1–2 is the answer to Romans 1:18–32.** The two passages share vocabulary, and that shared vocabulary is the lesson's argument. The class can check it in any interlinear.

| Romans 1 (the exchange) | Romans 12 (the renewal) |
|---|---|
| People *worshiped and served* (*elatreusan*) the creature rather than the Creator (1:25) | Present your bodies to God; this is your *worship* (*latreia*) (12:1) |
| God gave them over to the *dishonoring of their bodies* (*sōmata*, 1:24) | Present your *bodies* (*sōmata*) as a holy, living sacrifice (12:1) |
| They did not see fit to *approve* (*edokimasan*) God, so God gave them over to a mind that fails the test (*adokimon noun*, 1:28) | Be renewed in your *mind* (*nous*) so that you can *test and approve* (*dokimazein*) God's will (12:2) |
| Futile thinking; claiming to be wise, they became fools (1:21–22) | Think *soberly* (*sōphronein*), not above what you ought (12:3) |

Read together: worship, body, and mind are the three things Romans 1 says humanity exchanged and Romans 12 says God restores. This is the strongest text-based answer to "why does Paul put bodies and minds on the same altar?"

**Four more observations from the Greek and the canon:**

1. **"Bodies" is plural; "sacrifice" is singular** (*ta sōmata* … *thysian*). Many bodies, one offering. Verses 4–5 spell out the same idea: many members, one body.
2. **"Reasonable service" (NET) / "spiritual worship" (ESV) translates *logikē latreia*.** English "spiritual" is easily heard as "non-physical," which is exactly the dualism under examination. In a sentence built around *bodies*, "reasonable" is the safer rendering: worship that fits what God made us to be. (Translations differ; tell the class so.)
3. **Hebrews 10 is already on your slides.** The once-for-all offering is "the offering of the *body* of Jesus Christ" (10:10), and 10:5 says "a *body* you prepared for me." The sacrifice that is finished was bodily. The response that follows is bodily too. This links Segment 1 directly to Segment 4.
4. **The "age" does the shaping.** "Do not be conformed" (*syschēmatizesthe*) pictures being pressed into an outward pattern (*schēma*) by something other than yourself; "transformed" (*metamorphousthe*) is a change of form from the inside. The verse assumes we are *already being shaped* by "this present age" (*aiōn*), usually without noticing. That is the doorway for the whole reflection.

**The created-order anchors** (use two or three, not all):
- *Made, not self-made:* Ps 100:3 (he made us, we belong to him); 1 Cor 6:19–20 ("you are not your own… glorify God in your body").
- *Image is embodied and intrinsic:* Gen 1:26–27 (image, male and female); Gen 2:7 (dust and breath, one living person); Gen 5:1–3 (image passed on through bodily generation); Gen 9:6 and Jas 3:9 (image-value survives the Fall and does not depend on capacity, age, or ability).
- *Creation is good and the body is not the problem:* Gen 1:31; 1 Tim 4:1–5 (forbidding marriage and foods is a demonic teaching because creation is good); Col 2:8, 2:20–23 (human tradition and bodily severity have "no value"); 1 John 4:2 (Jesus Christ has come in the flesh).
- *The body has a future:* Rom 8:11, 23; 1 Cor 15:42–44; Phil 3:21. The hope is a *raised body*, not a released soul.
- *Male and female are created, distinct, and interdependent:* Gen 1:27; 2:18–24; Matt 19:4 (Jesus argues "from the beginning"); 1 Cor 11:11–12; 1 Cor 12:12–27 (members with different functions, none dispensable); Rom 12:4–8 (not all the members have the same function). **Distinct function is not lesser worth.** Plant this here and leave the church-order application for Week 11.

## 2. A frame that will hold up: two errors, one root

A starting list (Enlightenment, Romanticism, Pietism, higher criticism, Nietzsche, Evangelicalism) is workable as a list of *influences*. As a statement that all of them diminish the physical body, it will not survive a sharp student. A sharper frame is this:

- **The ghost error.** The real *me* is the mind, the inner self, the feeling; the body is a vehicle or a shell. (Descartes' soul/body split; Kant's rational autonomy as the ground of dignity; the Romantic "authentic inner self"; liberal theology's gospel of the "infinite value of the human soul" [Harnack's own wording]; and, in popular piety, "spiritual" meaning "non-physical," and heaven as disembodied.)
- **The machine error.** The body is only matter: mechanism, raw material, a thing with no given purpose (La Mettrie's *Man a Machine*; scientism; Nietzsche's will-to-power naturalism, where the body is exalted *but only as the raw material of the will*).
- **The shared root: autonomy.** Both errors agree that the body carries no Creator-given meaning, so the self supplies the meaning. That is Genesis 3:5 (deciding good and evil for oneself) and Romans 1:25 (the creature in the Creator's place). It also connects straight back to the course thesis: *the Good is God's character, not something we define.*
- **How it shows up now:** Carl Trueman's *The Rise and Triumph of the Modern Self* traces "expressive individualism" through Rousseau, the Romantics, and Nietzsche; Nancy Pearcey's *Love Thy Body* argues that a "fact/value split" divides the human being into a body (a lower-story "fact") and a person (an upper-story "value"). You do not need to name either author in class. They are useful background for you, and the ideas stand on the Scripture above.

### Cautions for the teacher

1. **Genealogies are plausible, not proven.** Historians debate how much any thinker caused any modern belief. Descartes' dualism was actually "interactionist" and he wrote at length on how mind and body affect each other. Pietism also produced Francke's orphanages and schools, and its heirs include much serious charity done *with bodies and hands*. Say "likely sources of what we absorbed," not "this is where it came from."
2. **Origin is not refutation.** An idea is not false because of its parentage (the genetic fallacy). The Berean question is always "what does the text say?"
3. **Model the humility yourself.** Historians of the Stone–Campbell Movement widely note the influence of Locke, Baconian induction, and Scottish Common Sense philosophy on Alexander Campbell's method, even where Campbell departed from Locke's conclusions. Telling the class, "my own tradition's way of reading Scripture has nineteenth-century roots too, and I have to test it the same way," will do more for the room than any argument. (Use your judgment on whether and how much to say.)
4. **Mixed-background room.** Some students came out of evangelical churches or still attend them. Frame it as "what we absorbed," not "what *they* believe." Grace first.

## 3. Narrative statements (written to be spoken)

*Use 4–5 of these. Suggested placements in brackets.*

**S1. [Open Segment 4, before the reading]**
"Nobody thinks from nowhere. Every one of us walked into this room with a way of seeing God, ourselves, and our bodies that somebody taught us. Most of it we absorbed without ever deciding to. Paul assumes exactly that, and the question he forces on us is whether we know who's been doing the teaching."

**S2. [After reading Rom 12:1]**
"Notice what Paul put on the altar: *bodies*. Not feelings. Not intentions. Not an idea of devotion. The same body Jesus offered in Hebrews 10 is the kind of thing we are asked to offer. If the sacrifice that saved us was a body, then the body isn't a shell to be endured. It is the material of worship."

**S3. [At Rom 12:2, "do not be conformed"]**
"Think of a mold. Something presses on soft material until it takes the shape of the mold. Paul says that's already happening: 'this age' has a shape, and it presses on us. The only way out is not to be pressed harder by a different mold. It's to be *transformed*, changed from the inside by renewal. And notice the verb: we submit to it. God does the renewing."

**S4. [The Romans 1 / 12 mirror]**
"Here's something that was hiding in plain sight. In Romans 1, people exchanged God for the creature, they dishonored their bodies, and God gave them up to a mind that failed the test. In Romans 12, the very same three words come back in reverse: worship, bodies, and a mind that can test and approve. Romans 12 is the repair of Romans 1."

**S5. [Two errors, one root]**
"There are two ways to get the body wrong. One says the *real you* is your mind or your inner feeling, and the body is just equipment you happen to be wearing. The other says the body is only matter with no purpose except what we assign it. They sound like opposites. But they agree on one thing: nothing in the body was *given*. We get to decide what it means. That's the oldest sentence in the Bible's story: 'you will be like God, knowing good and evil.'"

**S6. [Intrinsic value]**
"Scripture grounds a person's worth in the image of God, and that doesn't switch on or off with ability, age, productivity, or feeling. Genesis 9 and James 3 still speak of it after the Fall. A newborn, a person with dementia, and a person in a wheelchair carry the same image as the strongest and sharpest person in the room. Any worldview that makes worth depend on capacity or self-perception has left Genesis."

**S7. [Male and female, brief; Week 11 pointer]**
"God made humanity male and female and called it very good. Both are the image. Paul's picture of the body in Romans 12 and 1 Corinthians 12 is *different members, different functions, no member dispensable.* In two weeks we'll look at what that means for how God's house is ordered. For today, hold onto this: different roles are not different worth. And God's design is something we *discover*, not something we assign."

**S9. [Grace guardrail, before discussion]**
"None of this is a way to earn anything. We're not auditing ourselves to be good enough for the altar. We're doing what Paul's 'therefore' invites: in light of mercy that's already been given, we ask God to keep renewing a mind he has already claimed."

## 4. Questions (a ladder: notice, name, test, offer)

### Level 1: Notice *(silent sentence-completions, 60 seconds; write privately)*
- "When I say 'spiritual,' I usually mean ______."
- "Real worship is when I feel ______."
- "When I picture heaven, I picture ______. (Is there a body in the picture?)"
- "'Be true to yourself' means ______."
- "I'm most confident that my body is ______ (mine? a tool? a gift? a problem? something I decide about?)."

### Level 2: Name the source *(pairs, 3 minutes)*
- "Pick one of your answers. Where did you first hear it: church, family, school, a song or film, social media, or Scripture?"
- "Which of my answers would I have a hard time defending from a chapter and verse?"
- "What do the people I trust most assume about the body, feelings, or 'the real me'? Did I ever decide to agree?"

### Level 3: Test it *(whole class, Berean-style; 4–5 minutes)*
- "Psalm 100:3 and 1 Corinthians 6:19–20 both say something about who owns us. What follows if both are true?"
- "If Hebrews 10:5 says God *prepared a body* for the Son, what does that tell us about whether bodies are good?"
- "Romans 1 and Romans 12 both mention bodies and minds. What does Paul think happens to the mind when worship goes wrong, and what restores it?"
- "Where do our usual words ('spirit,' 'soul,' 'heart,' 'mind,' 'body') in Scripture describe the whole person, not just a part? (Rom 12:1 'bodies'; Rom 6:13 'members'; Luke 10:27 love God with 'heart, soul, strength, mind.')"

### Level 4: Offer it *(one question to carry home)*
- "What is one assumption I need to bring to the altar this week, and let the Word test?"
- "What part of my embodied life (schedule, sleep, appetite, work, marriage or singleness, aging, illness) have I treated as 'secular' or 'just mine' rather than offered to God?"

## 5. Where this connects in the course

- **Week 1 / 2:** The Good is God's character; Genesis 3:5 is the counterfeit (defining good for ourselves).
- **Week 3:** Good intentions fail because we substitute our judgment for God's word. Worldview assumptions are the invisible form of that substitution.
- **Week 5:** The Berean method is the test; this supplement supplies new things to test.
- **Week 11:** The anthropology planted here (made, embodied, male and female, distinct and indispensable) is what makes created order in the assembly intelligible. Don't litigate church order in Week 9.
