# Week 5 Teacher Guide — The Berean Method: Discerning the Sacred and the True

*The Obedience of Faith, Week 5 of 12. Unit II (Discernment). Companion to `Week05-TheBereanMethod.pptx` (7 slides, 20 click-stops; slide 4 is a full table, shown whole). Scripture from the NET Bible.*

**Aim:** Teach the twin disciplines — eager reception and daily examination — as the training regimen that produces the Hebrews 5:14 discernment the whole course has been pointing toward.

**What this week must accomplish (and nothing more):** the class leaves able to name both halves of the Berean posture — *soft heart, hard questions* — able to say why it must be *daily*, and holding one liberating permission: they may test what they hear, including in this room. This is the course's methodological center. Every prior week ended with "read like a Berean"; this is the week that cashes the phrase. If the class walks out treating daily Scripture reading as *training* rather than *duty*, the week succeeded.

**Where this sits in the arc:** Week 4 secured the gift (grace, given-righteousness). This week teaches how to tell the gift from its counterfeits — which is why it opens with the question of whether the sacred can be *known* at all, and answers from 1 Corinthians 2 before teaching any technique. Order matters: discernment is a Spirit-given *faculty* before it is a human *skill*. Teach that sequence or the whole thing curdles into pride.

## Segment 1 — The Sacred Under Pressure (0:00–12:00 · Slide 2)

**Name the pressure the class actually lives under** (first click). In our culture, religion gets quietly filed under "personal belief" — non-rational, unscientific, subjective, *true-for-you-not-for-me*. Your class feels this every day, from coworkers and children and the general air. Paul met the ancient version head-on: Jews demanded signs, Greeks demanded wisdom, and the cross looked like foolishness to both (1 Cor 1:22–23). The demotion of the gospel to "opinion" is not new.

**Paul's answer: the gospel is power, and it is revealed** (second click). Romans 1:16–17 — "not ashamed… it is God's *power* for salvation… the righteousness of God is *revealed*." Two claims the class needs: the gospel *does* something (power, not just information), and it comes *down* (revealed, not reasoned up to). "From faith to faith" — faith is the receiving organ, the way the ear is the organ for sound.

**The crucial epistemology** (third click, 1 Cor 2:7–14). This is the segment's heart, so slow down. Spiritual truth is *spiritually discerned* — it is received through a God-given faculty, not deduced from outside. To the person without that faculty, the things of the Spirit read as "foolishness" — and note carefully *why*: not because he is stupid or wicked, but because "he cannot understand them" (2:14) — he lacks the organ. This is why an ordinary believer can grasp what a brilliant skeptic cannot. It is not IQ; it is the Spirit.

**The bridge — and the guard against pride** (fourth click). Because the eye is *given* by the Spirit, the Berean method is not a clever human technique bolted onto the Bible. It is the Spirit-given eye being *exercised*. Say this plainly, because the danger of a discernment lesson is that it breeds arrogance — "I can spot error, I'm sharp." No: we discern by grace, the same way we're saved by grace (Week 4). The humble reader discerns better than the proud scholar. The obedience of faith (Rom 1:5) is how the sacred comes into focus — a submitted heart sees what a proud mind misses.

**Transition:** "So what does exercising that eye look like in practice? Luke hands us a portrait."

## Segment 2 — The Bereans: Soft Heart, Hard Questions (12:00–25:00 · Slide 3)

**Set the contrast** (first click). Paul preaches in Berea, having just been run out of Thessalonica. Luke grades the *hearers* — the Bereans were "more noble" (*eugenes*, well-born, of good character) than the Thessalonians. Not smarter. Nobler — in how they *received truth*. Two habits, held together, earn the word.

**Half one — Eagerly received: the soft heart** (second click). Not arms-crossed. Not "impress me." They *wanted* it to be true; they came with appetite and humility, ready to receive. **Do not skip or rush this half** — it is the one a room full of careful, doctrinally-minded adults will undervalue. Examination without appetite is not nobility; it is just cynicism wearing a Bible. The Bereans leaned in.

**Half two — Examined daily: the hard questions** (third click). They "examined the scriptures carefully every day to see if these things were so." Sit on the astonishing part: **they fact-checked an apostle.** Paul — inspired, Spirit-filled, writing-Scripture Paul — and they went home and checked him against the text, daily. And Luke does not scold them for insubordination. He calls them *noble*. Heaven's assessment of people who verify the preacher against Scripture is commendation. Let that land, because it sets up the permission you'll give in discussion: they may test *you*.

**The two failure modes** (fourth click). Most of us fail on exactly one side, and naming both lets everyone locate himself (this seeds discussion Q1):
- *All heart, no examination* — carried along by whoever preached last, blown by every wind of doctrine (Eph 4:14), warm and gullible.
- *All examination, no heart* — suspicion as a personality, nothing ever lands or changes them, cold and safe and unfed.

**Both at once** (fifth click). The noble hearer is *ready to believe and unwilling to be fooled* — simultaneously. And the paradox that resolves it: he is eager *because* the truth is precious, and examining *because* it is precious. The same love drives both halves. A man who guards his money carefully and also loves spending it on his family is not conflicted — he treasures it, which is why he both protects and enjoys it. So with truth.

**Transition:** "For the examining half, Scripture hands us a whole toolkit."

## Segment 3 — The Verification Toolkit (25:00–37:00 · Slide 4, the table)

The five texts appear as a grid, shown whole, so the class sees a *kit* rather than a list. Walk it, roughly two minutes per row.

- **1 Thessalonians 5:21** — "examine all things; hold fast to what is good." The goal of testing is *keeping the good*, not debunking. Testing is sorting, not cynical rejection — you test the field to find the gold, not to prove there's no gold. This guards the whole toolkit from becoming an engine of suspicion.
- **1 John 4:1** — "do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits." Spiritual *experiences* must be tested too — a feeling, a prophecy, an impression, a "God told me." Feelings are not self-authenticating. John gives the assay in the next verses: does it confess Christ come in the flesh (4:2–3)? Content, not intensity, certifies a spirit.
- **2 Timothy 2:15** — "accurately handling the word of truth." *Orthotomeō*, cutting straight. Accuracy is a *labor* ("be diligent") and a *duty*. Sloppy handling of Scripture is not humble simplicity; it is a failure of stewardship. This dignifies the hard work of study.
- **Colossians 2:8** — "no one captures you through philosophy and empty deceit." Persuasive is not the same as true — echo Week 3's "it works." The test Paul gives: is it "according to Christ" or "according to human tradition"? Watch the *source*, not just the polish.
- **Hebrews 5:14** (the gold row) — the course's controlling verse, finally in its home context. Point out what makes it different from the other four: they are *commands to do*; this one is the *result of doing them*. Perception "trained by practice." The first four are the reps; this is the muscle they build.

**The takeaway note:** four commands to test and one promise — do the testing long enough and the eye itself gets trained. The toolkit is not a checklist you run once; it is a gym you show up to.

**Transition:** "Which is exactly why it has to be *daily*."

## Segment 4 — Why Daily: Perception Is Trained (37:00–45:00 · Slide 5)

This segment pays off the entire course's controlling verse, so give it full weight even though it's late in the hour.

**Hebrews 5:14 in full** (first click). "Trained by practice." Say the three words twice, slower the second time. Not downloaded at baptism. Not absorbed from a good sermon. *Trained.* By *practice.*

**The tradesman illustration** (second click). Use the trade your class actually contains — carpenter, nurse, mechanic, rancher, electrician. The master spots the flaw without measuring; the nurse reads the patient at a glance; the rancher knows the sick animal across the field. Nobody downloaded that perception. They apprenticed — reps, daily, for years — until perception knew things the mouth couldn't yet explain. Expert perception is *built*, and it runs faster than conscious reasoning. That is precisely the word Hebrews chose for telling good from evil.

**Name the reps — the beautiful part** (third click). What are the repetitions that train spiritual perception? *Watching God draw the line between accepted and rejected worship, across hundreds of texts.* Cain and Abel. Nadab. Saul. Uzzah. Malachi. Ananias. The Pharisees — many of which this class has *already studied* in Weeks 1–4. Every judgment-scene in Scripture is another rep. The daily reader is calibrating his own eye to God's actual judgments until his instinct begins to match God's verdict. This reframes daily Bible reading from a devotional chore into perceptual training with the best possible coach.

**The engine, not the slogan** (fourth click). This is why every week of this course ends the same way — "read like a Berean" is not a tagline; it is the *mechanism*. Then the sober line, tying back to Week 3: the man not in the Word daily isn't merely under-informed — he's missing the *training data*, sincere and uncalibrated, and (Uzzah) will build new carts his whole life without ever feeling the wheels wobble. Land it: one chapter a day, eager and examining, is how the eye is built. Straight into discussion.

## Segments 5–6 — Discussion and Close (45:00–57:00 · Slides 6–7)

**Q1 — "Which half is harder for you — receiving eagerly, or examining daily? What does your harder half cost you?"** The self-locating question set up on slide 3; most people know their default instantly. Name the *cost* of each, gently: the gullible-leaning get led astray (they need the courage to question); the cynical-leaning never get fed (they need the humility to receive). This is tender for the cynics especially — the careful, discerning adult who quietly hasn't been *moved* by God's word in years. Grace, not scolding.

**Q2 — "What was the last teaching you actually tested against Scripture — not just absorbed? How?"** Push past the reflexive "I test everything" (nobody does) to a *specific instance* — a sermon, a book, a podcast, a viral claim, a thing a friend said God told them. If someone genuinely can't recall testing anything, that is the honest and useful answer, and the invitation for the coming weeks.

**The permission** (final click). Explicitly invite the class to test *this class* — including you — against the text. Modeling this in Week 5 is what makes the contested weeks (10–11, church order) safe and honest later: you will have already established that you *want* to be checked. A teacher who fears examination teaches the opposite of this lesson.

**The close (slide 7, ~2 min).** Next week's reading is Genesis 3:1–13 (flag it: the serpent attacks *the word* first — "did God really say?" — not Eve's morals; a discernment failure, which is why it follows this week) and 1 Corinthians 13 (what love does with truth, v.6). Preview Week 6. Carry sentence — say it together. Close on 1 Thessalonians 5:21. Dismiss on time.

## Answers to Questions You May Get

**"If I test the preacher, aren't I being proud or divisive?"** The opposite — Luke calls the Bereans *noble* for testing an apostle (Acts 17:11). Testing is not the same as attacking; the Bereans tested Paul *because they were eager to receive*, and having tested, they believed (17:12). Pride tests to win; nobility tests to obey. The divisive person tests to elevate himself; the noble person tests to make sure he's building on rock. Do it with a soft heart and it is faithfulness, not rebellion.

**"How can spiritual truth be 'foolishness' to smart people? Plenty of brilliant unbelievers understand the Bible better than I do."** They can understand it as *literature and history* — the facts, the arguments, the Greek. What 1 Corinthians 2:14 says they cannot do is *receive* it — welcome it as true and precious and life-commanding. A music critic who is tone-deaf can analyze a score perfectly and never hear the song. The faculty in view isn't intelligence; it's the Spirit-given capacity to recognize God's voice as God's (John 10:27). This should make the class humble, not smug — the same grace that gave them the ear could give it to that skeptic tomorrow.

**"Doesn't testing everything lead to endless doubt and never resting in anything?"** Note the second half of 1 Thessalonians 5:21 — "*hold fast* to what is good." Testing has a *terminus*: you test in order to *keep*, then you rest in what passed. The Berean method is not perpetual suspicion; it's due diligence that ends in settled conviction. The Bereans examined *and then believed* (Acts 17:12). Testing that never lands anywhere isn't the Berean method — it's the cynical failure mode from slide 3.

**"What do I do when my examination and my church's teaching disagree?"** First, suspect yourself before your teachers — humility is part of the soft heart, and most beginners who think they've found an error have found their own gap. Second, do the work: is this a clear command of Scripture, or a disputable matter (Rom 14)? Third, take it to mature believers and reason from the text, not from feeling (this is the coming weeks' method). The goal is never to win; it's to be right with God. Flag that Weeks 10–11 model exactly this on a genuinely contested topic.

**"Isn't 'the Spirit gives discernment' just a way to claim my opinions are God's?"** A fair and important worry — and Scripture guards against it precisely by tethering the Spirit's discernment to the *written word*. The Bereans didn't test Paul against their inner impressions; they tested him against the *Scriptures* (Acts 17:11). The Spirit who inspired the text does not contradict the text (that's the 1 John 4 test). Any "leading" that pulls away from what is written is failing the very test this week teaches. Spirit and word together — never word-less spirit, never spirit-less letter.
